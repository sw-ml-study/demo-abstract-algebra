Learn abstract algebra by breaking it

No prior algebra needed. Start by clicking on a grid.

Abstract algebra is usually taught as definitions you are asked to accept before you have seen anything they describe. This site does the reverse. You get a small grid of numbers, you change it, and software tells you what you have just built — and what you have just broken.

The whole subject sits on one idea. Pick a few things, and a rule that combines any two of them into a third. Write out what every pair gives you. That grid is the mathematical object; everything else is noticing which patterns the grid does and does not have.

WHAT YOU WILL LEARN

Five questions, asked of any rule. Each "yes" is a rung on a ladder:

  1. Closed — does combining two of your things always give you back one of your things?
  2. Associative — does it matter where you put the brackets?
  3. Identity — is there one element that leaves everything else alone?
  4. Inverses — can every move be undone?
  5. Commutative — does the order of the two inputs matter?

Those answers have names. A rule that is merely closed is a magma. Add associativity and it is a semigroup; add an identity, a monoid; add inverses, a group — the structure behind symmetry, and the reason this subject shows up in physics, cryptography and machine learning. You will meet each name only after you have already needed it.
